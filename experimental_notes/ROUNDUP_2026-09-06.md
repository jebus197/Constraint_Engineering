PART 1. THE BIGGEST RESULT: THE RUBRIC CONFLICT WAS NOT A CONFLICT

Decision 2 sent the rubric question to CC2 and Fable. Both seats came back and refuted the premise of my own brief, independently, and they agree.

The claim was that the rubric and the numeric cut of 0.7 disagree on 45.56 percent of cases. That number is real. What is wrong is calling it a disagreement.

All 259 judgeable cases sit inside the narrow band from 0.65 to 0.74. The audit was a boundary sample by construction, so the figure says nothing about how the number behaves anywhere else. Inside that band, CC2 measured Cohen's kappa at minus 0.0227, with a confidence interval from minus 0.159 to plus 0.114, and a Fisher exact p of 0.78. The two are not diverging. They are statistically unrelated. If they were entirely independent you would expect 44.55 percent disagreement, and the measured value is 45.56 percent.

CC2 then found the mechanism, which is the part that matters. Using 273 pairs where two different models independently scored the same defect, the per-assignment noise in the severity number has a standard deviation of 0.1419, bootstrap interval 0.1241 to 0.1575. The audit band is 0.09 wide. The instrument's own noise is larger than the decision it is being asked to make. Same defect, two models, opposite sides of the 0.7 line: 82 of 273, which is 30.04 percent, interval 24.9 to 35.7 percent.

A forward simulation using only that noise figure and the corpus distribution, with nothing fitted to the audit, predicts 45.58 percent. The measured value is 45.56 percent.

CC2 killed the two alternative explanations rather than assuming them away. Restricted range alone does not produce it: at zero noise the simulation gives 0.00 percent disagreement. A precise number with a badly placed cut does not produce it either, because that predicts one-sided errors, and the measured split is 67 over-calls against 51 under-calls with a binomial p of 0.167.

The consequence is important and it is good news: the 0.7 cut is not the fault, and it does not move. No new dated pre-registration is needed.

Fable came at it from the other side and agreed. The number carries no information inside the band: the area under the curve for severity predicting a critical rubric verdict is 0.464, where 0.5 is chance. Meanwhile the two blind readers agree with each other 92.5 percent of the time, kappa 0.837. In the band the rubric is reproducible and the number is noise.

Question 6, what the queue alarm does. When a finding cannot be settled by any tool it goes to an irreducible queue. If that queue exceeds a bound, currently 2, the alarm halts the run outright, prints a notification and attaches the per-finding evidence to the report. It was made harder deliberately, because it had been suppressed twice on 1 August by raising the bound while it was right both times. The cost is that it halted both experiment 55 attempts at round 1, which is why nothing has run for 12 days. My recommendation of veto-only rather than halt stands, but note the history: the last 2 people to soften it were wrong.

Question 8, what a target-independent falsifier is. A falsifier is a small program that tries to break a claim. The runner records a finding as confirmed when the falsifier fails, but it has never required the falsifier to actually read the document it accuses. So a falsifier that simply prints the word falsified is recorded as confirming a defect. The test is to replace the target with unrelated but valid content and re-run: if it still confirms, it was never reading the target. Of 372 archived falsifiers, 8 are provably in that class.

It is currently absent from your configuration, so the default applies and you get prompted. Setting it true removes the prompt. There is no consequence beyond that.
