# Asking five models how to tell a wrong accusation from a right one

2026-08-12, just before two in the morning. Five models, one round of questions, about three pounds.


## Why this question was put to a panel

The system works by having several independent models review a technical document and accuse it of faults. An accusation only counts if the accusing model also writes a small program that proves the fault, and the machinery runs that program itself. What a model says in prose decides nothing. Only what the program does when it runs decides anything.

That design has an obvious weak point. What happens when a program fails for a reason that has nothing to do with the fault it was supposed to prove? A missing file, a typo, a wrong path. The failure looks the same from the outside, and the accusation gets recorded as proven.

The panel was asked how to tell those two apart, using only calculations the machinery can perform on its own. A hard condition was attached to the question. No answer was allowed to work by asking models to vote, or to judge, or to express confidence. Everything proposed had to be computable and had to be itself testable.

The five models were run without any requirement to agree with one another. Each gave its own verdict and its strongest attack on the positions it expected the others to take. Where they disagree, that disagreement is kept, because it carries information that averaging would destroy.


## An accident that turned into the most useful result

The question sent to the panel contained a statement that was false, and nobody knew it at the time. It said the control document was believed to contain no errors, so any serious finding against it must be a false accusation.

Later the same night that turned out to be wrong. The document does contain real errors. This is covered in the companion note on the control document.

Four of the five models rejected that statement anyway, without being prompted, purely from reading the question. One accepted it, and produced the weakest answer of the five as a direct result. It dismissed a whole mechanism on the strength of the false statement.

That is about as clean a demonstration as one could ask for of why models should not be pushed toward agreement. Had the panel been run under a requirement to converge, the four correct rejections would have been pressure to move toward the one that accepted it.


## Where all five agreed

There was unanimous support for one idea in particular, and it happens to be an idea the project's founder has raised repeatedly since the beginning of August without it yet being built.

The idea is this. Rather than trying to work out what a proof meant by reading the words around it, require the proof to state its numbers plainly. What value did the document claim. What value did the calculation actually produce. Hand those two numbers back in a fixed format, and let the machinery compare the numbers rather than interpret the prose.

Every one of the five models proposed this independently, in slightly different forms, and every one of them put it near the top of its list. Five frontier models given the problem cold reached for the same answer the founder had already reached.

There was also unanimous agreement on three further points. That a failing proof should be classified by where it failed, whether during setup or during the actual check, rather than by what words the author happened to use in the error message. That the rate at which this system produces wrong accusations has never actually been measured. And that the current control document should eventually be replaced by one whose every claim is generated by a script, so that its correctness is a property of the generator rather than something its authors assert.


## The strongest single objection, and it lands on a recommendation already made

Before the panel ran, a recommendation had been made to the founder. It was that if a proof never even opens the document it is accusing, the accusation should be blocked from closing and sent to a human instead.

One panel model destroyed that recommendation in a sentence. A rule that checks whether a proof opened a file, rather than whether its conclusion actually depends on the contents, is one line of code away from being defeated. A model that learns its accusations get rejected simply adds a line that opens the file and throws the contents away. The measurement then reports perfect compliance, the actual discriminating power is zero, and the number moves in the direction that looks like success.

That last part is the serious bit. The failure would not announce itself. It would look like an improvement.

The recommendation is therefore withdrawn. The check is still worth having, because it can be computed for every proof rather than the one in five the current mechanism manages, but it must never be allowed to block anything on its own. It is a floor, not a judge.


## What should replace it

The same model proposed a much better instrument, and the reason it is better comes down to one observation.

The existing mechanism tries to establish whether a proof is really testing the fault it names by repairing the fault and seeing whether the proof goes quiet. That requires knowing the correct version, which requires knowing the answer, which is precisely what is not available.

The replacement does not need a corrected version. It needs a disturbed one, which needs nothing at all.

The machinery takes the passage under accusation and mechanically perturbs it. Separately, it perturbs an unrelated passage elsewhere in the same document. It then runs the proof against each and reads the result off a short table. If the verdict changes when the accused passage changes, and holds steady when an unrelated passage changes, the proof genuinely depends on what it accuses. If the verdict is unmoved by changes to the accused passage, the proof is not testing it. If everything changes it, the proof is testing nothing in particular. And if nothing changes it and the proof keeps failing regardless, the failure is in the equipment rather than the document.

That last row solves the older problem for free. A setup failure is by definition unaffected by the document's contents, and being unaffected is measurable without reading anybody's error message.

The instrument can also be checked against itself, which is what makes it admissible here. Feed it a proof known to be good against a fault known to be real, and it must show sensitivity. Feed it a proof that simply fails unconditionally, and it must show indifference. If those two do not come apart, the instrument is broken and says so out loud.

Two other models proposed the same shape of solution independently.


## Two things the panel found that had not been noticed

The first is that an earlier run of the same experiment can serve as a free measurement of how often the panel is right. Seven claims in the control document were repaired at the start of August. Those repairs happened because the faults were real and were present when the earlier run was carried out. So the earlier run's accusations can be checked against them. This was measured immediately and it holds. The panel had been right nearly every time.

The second concerns the rule that decides when a review is allowed to finish. That rule counts serious findings. But it counts findings as they were rated when first raised, not as they stood after the machinery had adjudicated them. The stopping decision is therefore driven partly by a quantity that nothing has yet checked. This is not an argument against the measure itself, which remains central and is not in question. It is a question about what is being fed into it, and it deserves a decision.


## Where the models disagreed, preserved

One model accepted the original framing entirely and placed every fault on the machinery rather than on the reviewing models. It also contradicted itself between two of its own answers, saying in one place that the document's correctness was merely assumed and in another that the count of confirmed findings gives the exact false accusation rate. Both cannot be true. It gave the shortest response of the five by a factor of two.

One model missed the false statement in the question and built on it, discarding a useful mechanism as a result. Its structured reporting proposal was nonetheless the most immediately implementable single suggestion any model made.

One model demolished the control document convincingly and then proposed a replacement scored by exactly the same flawed reasoning it had just taken apart.

And one model, while explaining how these proofs are supposed to work, wrote an example that has its logic exactly backwards. Its sample proof passes when the fault is real, which is the opposite of the rule it was describing in the same paragraph.

None of these are reasons to discount the panel. They are the reason the responses are kept in full rather than reduced to a consensus.


## What should be built, and in what order

Three mechanisms, in this order.

First, the structured statement of numbers. It is the cheapest, it has unanimous support, it is the founder's own long standing proposal, and every other mechanism becomes easier once proofs report their numbers instead of describing them.

Second, the perturbation instrument. It should be built before the final experiment rather than after, because it is the only proposal that resolves the underlying problem without needing an answer key.

Third, the file access check, demoted to a recorded observation with an explicit note that it can be defeated and must never block anything by itself. Recording a weak instrument as weak is worth more than discarding it, because it is the only one that covers every case.


## A note on method

The panel was given measurements rather than conclusions, and was openly invited to attack them. It did. The single most valuable thing it produced was not any of the mechanisms above. It was four models independently refusing to accept a statement the question had handed them as settled fact.
