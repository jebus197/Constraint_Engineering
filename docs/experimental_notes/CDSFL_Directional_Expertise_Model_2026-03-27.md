# Directional Expertise: A New Model of Human-AI Collaboration

**27 March 2026**

---

## The Observation

Over 13 days, a founder who describes himself as "not very good at maths" directed three AI models to produce a verified 10-component mathematical framework. He did not write any of the formulas. He did not derive any of the proofs. He did not perform any of the computations.

What he did was tell the models what to compute, recognise when the output was wrong, connect results across domains that the models treated as separate, and judge when the work was finished.

The framework is computationally verified. Every formula has been tested against concrete examples. Every edge case has been checked. The mathematics is sound. And the person who directed its creation cannot do the mathematics himself.

This appears to demonstrate something worth examining carefully.

---

## What the Founder Contributed

**Pattern recognition.** He looked at a single data set showing a model's findings decreasing across review rounds (5, 3, 2, 2, 0) and said "this looks like the inverse square root law." He was right. The pattern connected to established statistical principles that the models had not identified from the same data.

**Falsification instinct.** When results appeared to show that the scientific method does not work, his immediate response was not to question the method but to question the experimental design. He was right every time. The design was wrong. The method was fine.

**Fairness judgment.** He identified when the test conditions were unfairly stacked: too much guidance for one condition, distributed compute given to the control group, directive asymmetry between models. These are judgment calls that require understanding what fairness means in an experimental context, not mathematical skill.

**Cross-domain connection.** He noticed that two apparently unrelated problems — the structure of quantum mechanics and the distribution of prime numbers — might point to the same underlying mathematical structure. Whether this connection is real remains to be tested. That he saw it at all is the contribution.

**Capability matching.** He recognised that different AI models have different strengths and assigned them accordingly: Gemini for mathematical formalisation, Codex for code analysis, Claude for orchestration, DeepSeek for volume work. This is team management, not mathematics.

None of these contributions required mathematical skill. All of them required knowing what questions to ask.

---

## What the AI Models Contributed

**Computation.** Deriving formulas, solving equations, evaluating expressions, fitting models to data.

**Precision.** Identifying edge cases, checking boundary conditions, verifying convergence properties.

**Breadth.** Searching literature, finding relevant prior work, connecting to established mathematical frameworks.

**Verification.** SymPy and Wolfram Alpha provided independent computational verification of every formula.

**Tirelessness.** Running hundreds of bench test conditions across multiple days without degradation.

None of these contributions required knowing what questions to ask. All of them required being told what to compute.

---

## The Model of Expertise

Traditional expertise means being able to **do** the work. A mathematician can derive proofs. An engineer can run structural analysis. A doctor can diagnose diseases. Expertise is measured by the ability to perform domain-specific tasks.

What this project suggests is a different model. **Directional expertise** means being able to **direct** the work: knowing what needs computing without being able to compute it, recognising when the output is wrong without being able to produce the correct output yourself, connecting results across domains without being a specialist in any of them.

This is not a new idea in principle. Managers direct work they cannot do themselves. Conductors direct orchestras they cannot play alone. Research directors set agendas for work they cannot personally execute. But in all those cases, the human directs other humans. What is potentially new here is a human directing AI systems as cognitive collaborators — where the AI provides computational capability the human lacks and the human provides directional capability the AI lacks.

The combination produces output that neither could produce alone. The founder could not have written the Duane model or the Bayesian multi-verifier or the Abstraction Index. The AI models could not have identified the decay curve pattern from raw data, challenged their own experimental design, or connected quantum mechanics to prime numbers. The framework exists because both contributions were present.

---

## What This Means for Expertise

If directional expertise is as valuable as computational expertise, and if AI tools provide the computational component, then the critical human skill becomes **methodology, not mathematics**. Knowing how to think about problems. Knowing what questions to ask. Knowing when something smells wrong. Knowing when to stop.

This is what the founder encoded in his directive set — the `CLAUDE.md` file that configures Claude's analytical behaviour. It contains no mathematics at all. It contains methodology: coupled generation-falsification, proportional rigour, hard/soft constraint classification, iterative refinement with stopping criteria, the obligation to push back on impossible claims. These are directional instructions, not computational ones.

The directive set worked. Under its direction, Claude produced the analytical framework. Under different directives (or no directives), the same model produces polished but unverified output, agrees with everything, and never challenges its own conclusions. **The directive set is the human's directional expertise encoded as a portable configuration.**

If this model is correct, then the most valuable human skill in the age of AI is not knowing how to compute answers but knowing how to ask questions. Not mathematical literacy but **methodological literacy**. Not the ability to solve problems but the ability to recognise which problems are worth solving and to judge whether the proposed solutions are genuine.

This does not diminish the value of mathematical skill. A human who can both direct and compute is more capable than one who can only direct. But it suggests that the barrier to producing verified mathematical work is lower than traditionally assumed, provided the human has strong methodological instincts and access to capable AI tools.

The founder demonstrated this. Whether it generalises to other people, other domains, and other levels of mathematical understanding is an empirical question that the framework itself provides the tools to test.

---

## Limitation

This observation is based on **one project by one person**. It would be overclaiming to generalise from a single case. The founder's directional contributions may reflect unusual pattern recognition ability (formally diagnosed autistic cognition optimised for structural relationships), strong methodological training (encoded in the directive set), or simply luck. Distinguishing these explanations requires testing the same methodology with different people of varying mathematical and methodological backgrounds.

What can be stated is that the output exists, is computationally verified, and was produced by someone who could not have produced it alone. That much is not a claim. It is a documented fact.
