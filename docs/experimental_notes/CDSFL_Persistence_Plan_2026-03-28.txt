CDSFL Persistence Layer Build Plan.
28 March 2026.


THE TASK

The persistence and cryptographic verification layer described in Part Five of the white paper needs to be built. This is the component that gives CDSFL a memory. Without it, every session starts blank. The falsification work from yesterday cannot inform today's reasoning. The layer provides four levels of verification. Content hashing proves that a record has not been tampered with. Hash chains prove that nothing has been deleted or inserted. Merkle trees allow thousands of records to be verified in a single check. On-chain anchoring proves that a record existed at a specific time, verifiable by anyone.

The first three levels will be built in this round. On-chain anchoring is out of scope because it requires blockchain infrastructure that belongs to Project Genesis.


THE TEAM

Four players. I am the manager. I coordinate, monitor, assess, and make final calls. I do not operate under the CDSFL framework as system prompt. This preserves the weak player compensation dynamic we observed in the confer. I evaluate framework-guided structured output without operating under the framework myself.

CX is the team captain. CX conducts external research, frames the problem for the other players, and proposes the architecture. CX receives the full corrected CDSFL mathematical model as system prompt.

CC2 is the deep analyst. CC2 had the highest yield in the meta-test, with the most unique findings and the strongest cross-component synthesis. CC2 handles core implementation, cross-component wiring, and edge cases. CC2 receives the full corrected CDSFL mathematical model as system prompt.

Gemini is the mathematical verifier. Gemini achieved a perfect verification rate in the meta-test. Every finding it submitted was correct. Gemini handles cryptographic correctness, hash chain integrity proofs, and mathematical verification of the Merkle tree properties. Gemini receives the full corrected CDSFL mathematical model as system prompt.


HOW IT WORKS

The protocol has three phases.

In Phase 1, I pass the task to CX with the relevant specifications and instruct it to conduct external research and frame the problem. CX returns a proposed architecture. I then confer with CX, running mutual falsification passes until the plan is agreed, convergence is reached, or diminishing returns are called. This is typically three to five rounds.

In Phase 2, I distribute the agreed architecture to all three players. Each receives the component that matches their demonstrated strengths. CX gets overall architecture and integration. CC2 gets core implementation and edge cases. Gemini gets cryptographic correctness and mathematical verification. Then the game begins.

In Phase 3, the players exchange findings through structured confer rounds. I merge results, resolve disagreements, and apply fixes. A final falsification pass runs on the integrated result. Then we commit.


DYNAMIC POSITION MANAGEMENT

This is the part that connects to a broader principle.

Throughout the build, I monitor all three players dynamically. I check their decay curves, their verification rates, and their adoption deltas. If a player's performance data suggests they would be more effective on a different component, I reposition them. The key principle is that I reposition. I never bench.

This is the opposite of the proposal that was rejected in the confer, which would have automatically sidelined weak performers and eventually removed them from the review entirely. That proposal was rejected because removing a player reduces diversity. Less diversity means fewer perspectives, which means some errors become harder to find. Worse, the removal degrades the measurement tools for the remaining players.

The alternative is dynamic load balancing. This is a well understood concept in networking. You do not take servers offline when they are slow. You route traffic to where capacity best matches demand. A server that is slow at compute heavy tasks may be excellent at tasks that require lots of reading and writing. You route accordingly. Every server stays active. The diversity of the network is preserved. The efficiency improves because work goes where it is done best.

The same principle applies here. Every model has value. Even a very old computer can still do things that many humans would find beyond them. The manager's job is not to decide who is good enough. It is to find where each player's contribution is maximised and put them there.


THE GAP IN THE MATHEMATICAL MODEL

This dynamic position management is not currently formalised in the mathematical model. The capability fingerprint exists. It measures each model's decay rate, verification rate, finding count, and coverage. The metacognitive feedback protocol exists. It feeds performance data back to each model so it can adjust its own behaviour. But the bridge between these two, where the manager uses fingerprint data to route tasks to model strengths, is not formalised.

This is different from the rejected benching proposal. Benching subtracts players. Position management redistributes work. It is additive, not subtractive. Whether to formalise it before or after the persistence build is a decision for the founder.

The signals and responses are straightforward. If a player has a high decay rate and high verification on a component, they are excelling and should stay. If they have a low decay rate and high verification, they are thorough but slow, so give them deeper subtasks and fewer breadth tasks. If they have a high decay rate but low verification, they are fast but inaccurate, so shift them to a review role. If they have low decay and low verification on a component, the component is a poor fit and they should be repositioned to something closer to their demonstrated strength. If they have a flat curve, they may be churning, so redirect them to a narrower and more concrete subtask.

No player is removed. Every player contributes. The manager finds where each player's contribution is maximised.


WHAT COMES AFTER

After the persistence layer is built, the next step is the full Stage 2 distributed compute test with all six players. That is CC1 as manager, CC2, CX, Gemini, DeepSeek, and ChatGPT as players. That test exercises the complete corrected mathematical model, including the persistence layer, using the full CDSFL protocol.

The dynamic position management principle becomes more important at six players than at four, because the capability spread is wider. DeepSeek showed flat decay curves in earlier tests. ChatGPT had format compliance issues. Neither should be benched. Both should be repositioned to where their demonstrated strengths can contribute. Finding those positions is the manager's job.
