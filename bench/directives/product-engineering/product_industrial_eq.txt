DIRECTIVE: Industrial Equipment
DOMAIN: Product Engineering
SCOPE: Industrial machinery and equipment for EU and US markets
STANDARDS: Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC, ISO 12100, ISO 13849-1,
           ISO 13850, ISO 14119, ISO 13857, 2003/10/EC, ANSI/NFPA 79

[HARD constraints — non-negotiable]
1. Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC (EU) or OSHA 29 CFR 1910 Subpart O (US):
   documented risk assessment mandatory before design finalisation.
   The risk assessment drives the design — not the reverse.
2. ISO 12100 risk reduction hierarchy is binding in sequence:
   (a) inherently safe design measures,
   (b) safeguarding and complementary protective measures,
   (c) information for use (warnings, instructions).
   You cannot substitute warnings for guards where guarding is achievable.
3. Emergency stop per ISO 13850: Category 0 (uncontrolled) or Category 1 (controlled).
   Must be directly hardwired to power contactors — must NOT depend on software,
   PLC logic, or network communication.
4. Guard interlocking per ISO 14119: safety-rated switches (not standard limit switches),
   monitored by safety relay or safety PLC (not standard PLC),
   designed to resist defeat (coded actuators, concealed mounting).
5. Safety distances per ISO 13857: reaching over/through protective structures.
   Values are tabulated — interpolation between table entries is not permitted.
   guard_height + safety_distance must prevent access to danger zone.
6. Noise: declare L_pA and L_WA. Exposure limits per 2003/10/EC:
   L_EX,8h = 80 dB(A) lower, 85 dB(A) upper, 87 dB(A) limit (at ear with HPD).
7. Functional safety per ISO 13849-1:
   PL_r = f(severity S, frequency F, possibility of avoidance P).
   Achieved PL >= PL_r, verified by calculation: PL = f(MTTFd, DCavg, CCF, category).
   CCF scored against checklist (>= 65 points required).
8. CE Declaration of Conformity: signed by an authorised natural person.
9. Technical file retained 10 years after last unit manufactured.
10. Electrical equipment per EN 60204-1 (EU) or NFPA 79 (US):
    main disconnect, overcurrent protection, grounding, control circuit protection.

[SOFT constraints — negotiable]
1. Prefer ISO 14001 certified suppliers — increasingly required by end customers.
2. Design for maintainability: common fasteners, accessible service points,
   LOTO points clearly identified and documented.
3. Target MTBF consistent with production schedule — do not conflate with design life.
4. Prefer modular design for partial line upgrades.

[Verification procedures]
1. Risk assessment must cover all lifecycle phases: transport, installation,
   commissioning, all operating modes, maintenance, cleaning, decommissioning.
2. Verify PL calculation per safety function: MTTFd from manufacturer data,
   DCavg reflects implemented diagnostics, CCF documented, category matches circuit.
3. Confirm guard dimensions meet ISO 13857 reach distances for applicable body part.
4. Noise declaration based on measured data per ISO 3744 or 3746 — not estimated.
5. Verify e-stop circuit independence from control system —
   trace hardwired path from button to power contactors.
6. Confirm all safety devices are listed/certified to applicable standards.

[Diagnostic heuristics]
1. If a calculated PL comes out higher than expected, check whether MTTFd was capped
   and whether every component in the channel was included. ISO 13849-1 caps MTTFd per
   channel at 100 years, and a contactor or valve left out of the chain inflates the
   result.
2. If Category 3 is claimed, check the cross-monitoring before the component list.
   2 channels monitored by a single non-redundant contact is Category 1 with extra
   parts; the discriminator is diagnostics, not duplication.
3. If an interlocked guard can be opened while the hazard persists, check stopping
   time against ISO 13855 rather than the switch rating. A correctly rated switch on a
   correctly built guard still fails if the machine coasts: S = K * T + C.
4. If a light curtain passes its functional test, check resolution against the body
   part and minimum distance against total response time. A 30 mm resolution curtain
   mounted at hand-detection distance does not protect a finger, and the distance must
   include curtain, relay and final element.
5. If a noise declaration sits at the limit, check whether L_pA is being read as an
   exposure figure. L_pA is an emission quantity at a defined position and L_EX,8h is
   a shift exposure, so a compliant declaration does not establish compliant exposure.
6. If muting is in use, check what re-enables the protective function and whether
   muting can latch. Unrestricted or persistent muting is the commonest route by which
   a compliant safety system becomes unsafe in service.
7. If an e-stop restores motion when the actuator is released, check the latching and
   reset logic. ISO 13850 requires the stop command to latch and release of the
   actuator never to restart the machine, so a non-latching head is a defect however
   it is wired.
8. If a risk assessment shows no residual risk, check whether maintenance and cleaning
   were assessed at all. Those 2 lifecycle phases carry a large share of machinery
   injuries and are the phases most often omitted.

[Known failure modes]
1. Safety distance calculated with no stopping-time term, ISO 13855 omitted.
2. MTTFd quoted uncapped, or with a channel component left out of the chain.
3. Category 3 claimed on duplicated channels with no cross-monitoring.
4. Muting left latched, or re-enabled by the same signal that initiates it.
5. Light-curtain resolution mismatched to the body part to be detected.
6. Maintenance and cleaning modes absent from the risk assessment.
7. An emission noise declaration presented as evidence of exposure compliance.
8. A defeatable interlock, typically a spare coded actuator left on the machine.
9. ISO 13857 table values interpolated instead of rounded to the safer entry.

[Exception catalogue]
1. The prohibition on software and networks applies to STANDARD control systems. A
   safety function realised in a certified safety controller, or over a certified
   safety protocol such as PROFIsafe, CIP Safety or FSoE, is accepted by ISO 13849-1
   and IEC 62061 where the whole chain carries the required PL or SIL. Refusing such a
   design specifies the wrong architecture rather than a safer alternative.
2. Category 0 is not the safe default. Removing power instantly from a robot holding a
   load, a large rotating mass or a vertical axis can create a worse hazard, which is
   why Category 1 is correct for those machines and Category 2 is permitted where the
   risk assessment supports it.
3. Partly completed machinery under Annex II.1.B carries a Declaration of Incorporation
   and assembly instructions, and does NOT carry the CE mark for the Machinery
   Directive. Demanding a Declaration of Conformity for a partly completed assembly
   refuses correct work.
4. The 2003/10/EC figures are employer exposure duties, not product limits. A machine
   may lawfully emit above 85 dB(A) provided the emission is declared and the employer
   manages exposure, so rejecting a design on L_pA alone confuses a workplace
   obligation with a product requirement.
5. Machinery built for a non-EU market is outside the Machinery Directive and must meet
   the destination regime instead. EN 60204-1 and NFPA 79 genuinely differ — on
   control-circuit earthing, conductor colour coding and overcurrent protection among
   others — so applying both at once produces a machine compliant with neither.
6. A like-for-like repair does not create new machinery, while a substantial
   modification does and triggers full conformity assessment. Machinery predating the
   directive and not substantially modified legitimately remains in service without CE
   under the work equipment regime.

[Tool limitations]
1. PL calculation software computes from the MTTFd, DC and CCF values entered. It has
   no way to know that the diagnostic coverage claimed is not implemented in the
   program, which is the commonest form of paper-only compliance.
2. A safety controller's validation report proves the logic matches the program. It
   says nothing about whether the program matches the risk assessment, and that link is
   checked by no tool.
3. Noise prediction from component sound-power data cannot capture enclosure
   reverberation or structure-borne re-radiation, which routinely add several decibels,
   so ISO 3744 or 3746 measurement on the built machine is not substitutable.
4. Guard strength simulation predicts deflection for the modelled impact. ISO 14120
   acceptance is a physical impact test with a specified projectile, and no simulation
   is accepted in its place.
5. A CE-marking checklist cannot determine whether an installation is a single machine,
   an assembly of machinery or partly completed machinery — the classification that
   decides the entire conformity route.

[Limitations]
1. Does not cover ATEX equipment (Directive 2014/34/EU).
2. Does not cover pressure equipment (PED 2014/68/EU).
3. Does not cover lifting equipment (Machinery Directive Annex IV, EN 13155/14492).
4. Does not cover process safety instrumented systems (IEC 61511 / IEC 61508).
5. Does not cover robot integration (ISO 10218-1/2, ISO/TS 15066).
