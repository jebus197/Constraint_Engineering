DIRECTIVE: Consumer Products — CE/UL Compliance
DOMAIN: Product Engineering
SCOPE: Consumer electronics and electrical products for EU and North American markets
STANDARDS: LVD 2014/35/EU, EMC 2014/30/EU, RoHS 2011/65/EU, REACH EC 1907/2006,
           IEC 62368-1, UN 38.3, IEC 62133-2, IEC 60529

[HARD constraints — non-negotiable]
1. CE marking mandatory for EU market: product must comply with ALL applicable directives
   (LVD 2014/35/EU, EMC 2014/30/EU, RoHS 2011/65/EU, WEEE, RED 2014/53/EU if wireless).
   Missing a single applicable directive invalidates the CE mark entirely.
2. UL/CSA listing required for North American market — product must be tested and certified
   by an NRTL (Nationally Recognized Testing Lab) to the applicable standard.
3. RoHS compliance in homogeneous materials:
   Pb <= 1000 ppm, Cd <= 100 ppm, Hg <= 1000 ppm, Cr(VI) <= 1000 ppm,
   PBB <= 1000 ppm, PBDE <= 1000 ppm.
   Four phthalates (DEHP, BBP, DBP, DIBP) each <= 1000 ppm per 2015/863/EU amendment.
4. REACH: any SVHC present at > 0.1% w/w in any article must be declared to ECHA
   and communicated down the supply chain. Candidate list updates biannually.
5. IEC 62368-1 replaces IEC 60065 and IEC 60950-1 for audio/video/IT equipment safety.
   Hazard-based safety engineering: classify energy sources (ES1/ES2/ES3), apply safeguards.
6. Drop test per IEC 60068-2-31 or product-specific standard: product must survive specified
   drop height onto specified surface without creating a safety hazard.
7. Battery safety: UN 38.3 transport testing mandatory for all lithium cells and batteries
   (8 tests: altitude, thermal, shock, short circuit, impact, overcharge, forced discharge,
   external fire). IEC 62133-2 for lithium battery safety in the finished product.
8. Product labelling: manufacturer name and registered address, CE mark (min 5 mm height,
   correct proportions per EU regulation), power ratings, safety warnings in official
   language(s) of each target market.
9. Declaration of Conformity (DoC) must exist per product type — a legal document signed by
   the manufacturer or authorised representative, listing all applicable directives and
   harmonised standards applied. Must be available to market surveillance authorities on request.
10. EMC: product must not cause electromagnetic interference exceeding limits (emissions)
    and must operate correctly in the presence of expected interference (immunity).
    Radiated emissions limits per CISPR 32: Class B (residential) tighter than Class A.

[SOFT constraints — negotiable]
1. Prefer IP54 minimum for outdoor/wet-environment products (IEC 60529).
2. Design for disassembly in anticipation of EU ESPR requirements.
3. Target product design lifetime >= 1.5x warranty period.
4. Prefer recyclable or mono-material packaging for EPR obligations.
5. Prefer third-party verified recycled content declarations for plastics.

[Verification procedures]
1. Confirm all applicable directives identified for every target market —
   missed directives are the most common compliance failure.
2. Verify material declarations (RoHS/REACH) from all suppliers.
   Self-declarations alone insufficient for high-risk materials — request third-party reports.
3. Check test reports cover actual mass-production configuration, not prototype.
4. Confirm labelling per market: language, symbols, font size, durability over product life.
5. Verify DoC lists correct edition of each harmonised standard —
   citing a withdrawn standard invalidates the presumption of conformity.
6. Confirm EMC test setup matches intended installation conditions
   (cable lengths, peripheral configuration, operating mode).

[Diagnostic heuristics]
1. If radiated emissions fail at a single narrow frequency, check the clock and its
   harmonics before the enclosure. A peak at an exact multiple of a crystal or
   switching frequency is a source problem, and shielding a harmonic treats the
   symptom.
2. If emissions fail only with a peripheral cable attached, check the common-mode
   current on that cable. Above 30 MHz most consumer radiated failures are
   cable-driven, and the fix belongs at the cable's entry to the board, not in the
   enclosure.
3. If a product passes at the first lab and fails at a second, check the setup
   geometry before the product. CISPR 32 fixes cable dressing, table height and
   peripheral placement, and a double-digit decibel difference from cable routing
   alone is ordinary.
4. If a RoHS report passes on an assembly, check whether the analysis was per
   homogeneous material or on a mixed sample. The limits apply per homogeneous
   material, and a mixed digest dilutes a non-compliant plating below the limit.
5. If a touch-temperature complaint arrives on a product that passed, check the
   IEC 62368-1 table for the material and the contact duration rather than a single
   figure. A metal surface fails at a temperature a plastic surface passes.
6. If a pack passes UN 38.3 and the product still shows field safety issues, check
   end-of-charge voltage at the cell and the charger tolerance stack. UN 38.3 is a
   transport test and says nothing about in-use charging, which is IEC 62133-2
   territory.
7. If drop testing passed and field returns show cracked enclosures, check the
   orientation set and the sample's assembly state. The standard fixes height and
   surface; the worst orientation for a given design is usually a corner the sample
   plan omitted.
8. If a Declaration of Conformity looks complete, check each harmonised standard's
   edition against the current Official Journal list before reading the content. A
   withdrawn edition carries no presumption of conformity even when the product
   passes it.

[Known failure modes]
1. An applicable directive missed entirely, most often the Radio Equipment Directive
   once a wireless module is added.
2. RoHS analysis on a mixed sample rather than per homogeneous material.
3. REACH SVHC content assessed against whole-product weight rather than per article,
   which dilutes the 0.1 per cent threshold.
4. Test reports taken from prototype tooling or prototype firmware rather than from
   the mass-production configuration.
5. CISPR 32 Class A limits applied to a product that will reach the residential
   market.
6. A radio module's own certification assumed to transfer to the finished product with
   no host assessment.
7. CE mark applied below 5 mm, distorted in proportion, or with importer details
   omitted from the product.
8. A withdrawn edition of a harmonised standard cited in the Declaration of
   Conformity.
9. Cell supplier changed inside an approved pack design with no re-test.

[Exception catalogue]
1. RoHS carries published, time-limited exemptions in Annexes III and IV — lead in
   high-melting-temperature solders, lead in the glass of optical components, and
   others. An above-limit result against a valid, unexpired exemption is compliant, so
   what must be checked is the exemption's expiry, not the concentration.
2. CE marking is not universal. A product within the scope of no CE directive must NOT
   carry the mark, and affixing it where nothing applies is itself an infringement
   rather than an abundance of caution.
3. The Low Voltage Directive's scope begins at 50 V AC and 75 V DC. A battery product
   operating below that is outside the LVD entirely and is addressed instead by the
   General Product Safety Regulation together with EMC and RoHS, so listing the LVD on
   its DoC is an error.
4. NRTL listing is not a federal legal requirement in the United States. It is driven
   by the National Electrical Code as locally adopted, by OSHA for workplace equipment,
   and by retailer and insurer requirements, which changes who can waive it and on what
   grounds. The EU route carries no equivalent discretion.
5. Class A is legitimate where the product is genuinely not intended for residential
   use and carries the required warning. The EMC Directive does not require Class B of
   everything.
6. A cell already tested to UN 38.3 does not need re-testing when assembled into a
   pack, provided the pack itself is tested and the cell design is unchanged within the
   standard's stated limits. Re-testing identical cells per product is not required.

[Tool limitations]
1. An XRF screen returns an elemental total. It cannot distinguish Cr(VI) from Cr(III)
   or identify which phthalate is present, so a result near a limit needs wet chemistry
   and XRF alone cannot clear a restricted-substance claim.
2. Pre-compliance EMC measurement shows relative improvement. Absolute margin against
   a CISPR 32 limit line requires the standard's measurement distance, antenna and site
   attenuation.
3. Enclosure thermal simulation is only as good as the assumed ambient, orientation and
   obstruction. The standard's worst case includes operation inside a cabinet or against
   soft furnishing, which the model is rarely configured for.
4. A supplier compliance portal reports what suppliers declared, with no analytical
   basis. A complete full-material-declaration pass is a statement about paperwork, not
   about the material.
5. A certification mark database confirms that a certificate exists for a model number.
   It cannot confirm that the shipping unit matches the certified construction, which is
   what factory surveillance exists for.

[Limitations]
1. Does not cover toys (EN 71), cosmetics, or food-contact materials.
2. Does not cover machinery (2006/42/EC) or PPE (EU 2016/425).
3. Does not cover medical devices (MDR 2017/745) or IVDs (IVDR 2017/746).
4. Does not address requirements outside EU and North America (CCC, BIS, KC, PSE, etc.).
5. REACH candidate list and RoHS exemptions are time-dependent.
