DIRECTIVE: Thermal-Electrical Interface
DOMAIN: Cross-Domain (Thermal + Electrical/Electronic)
SCOPE: Thermal management of power-dissipating electronic components on PCBs and heatsinks
STANDARDS: IPC-2152, IPC-SM-785, JEDEC JESD51 series, MIL-HDBK-217

[HARD constraints — non-negotiable]
1. Thermal resistance network: T_j = T_a + SUM(P_i * R_theta(i)).
   Cumulative through ALL resistances (jc, cs, sa). None may be omitted.
2. Multiple sources on shared heatsink — thermal superposition:
   T_j(k) = T_a + P_k*R_theta(jc_k) + P_k*R_theta(cs_k) + SUM(P_i)*R_theta(sa)
   Every device contributes to heatsink rise. Ignoring neighbours underestimates T_j.
3. Electrical derating with temperature:
   MOSFET R_DS(on) increases ~0.4%/C (positive temperature coefficient).
   IGBT V_CE(sat) has positive temp coefficient above crossover point.
   Electrolytic capacitor ESR increases and ripple current derates with temperature.
   Follow manufacturer derating curves — not nominal values.
4. Thermal runaway condition:
   dP_diss/dT > dP_cooling/dT
   If dissipation increases faster with temperature than cooling removes,
   system is thermally unstable. Evaluate at worst-case operating point.
5. Thermal time constants: tau = R_theta * C_th.
   Steady-state R_theta alone is insufficient for pulsed loads.
   Use Cauer or Foster RC network for transient impedance Z_theta(t).
   Datasheet Z_theta(t) is typically single-pulse — duty cycle correction required.
6. FR4 is a thermal insulator: k_FR4 ~ 0.3 W/(m*K) through-plane.
   Copper planes and thermal vias are the primary vertical heat paths.
   1 oz (35 um) copper plane: ~50-70 C/W per cm^2 — insufficient alone above ~1W.
7. Solder joint thermal fatigue (Coffin-Manson):
   N_f proportional to (delta_epsilon)^(-n), n ~ 2-3 for SnAgCu.
   Strain driven by CTE mismatch and delta_T.
   Larger components (greater DNP) experience greater strain per cycle.
8. Component reliability vs temperature (Arrhenius):
   AF = exp[(Ea/k) * (1/T_use - 1/T_test)], Ea ~ 0.7 eV typical.
   Failure rate approximately doubles every 10-15 C increase in T_j.

[SOFT constraints — negotiable]
1. Prefer thermal via arrays under power devices:
   0.3 mm drill, 1.0 mm pitch, filled/capped if soldered above.
   Reduces through-board R_theta by 3-5x vs bare FR4.
2. Prefer aluminium-core or IMS PCBs for LED and power applications.
3. Target T_j,max - T_j,operating >= 25 C margin for manufacturing and ageing.
4. Prefer exposed-pad packages (QFN, PowerPAD) over leaded (SOIC, QFP) above 0.5W.
5. Prefer TIM with bulk conductivity >= 1 W/(m*K) and controlled bond-line thickness.

[Verification procedures]
1. Calculate T_j for each power device at worst-case ambient AND full load.
   Use worst-case R_theta values (not typical).
2. Verify derating at calculated T_j, not nominal ambient.
   This is circular: P depends on T, T depends on P. Iterate or solve simultaneously.
3. Check thermal coupling between adjacent devices on shared substrates.
4. Confirm transient Z_theta(t) adequate for pulsed profiles — duty cycle corrected.
5. Solder fatigue life (Coffin-Manson or Engelmaier model) must exceed
   required product lifetime with safety factor >= 2.

[Diagnostic heuristics]
1. T_j converged in a single pass: check whether the derating was applied at all.
   R_DS(on) rising ~0.4%/C makes P a function of T, so a calculation that
   did not iterate has assumed a constant-power device by construction.
2. Thermal model stable at every steady-state operating point and the hardware fails
   in service anyway: test the SLOPE condition dP_diss/dT against dP_cooling/dT. A
   fixed point can exist and be unstable, and solving for the fixed point cannot see
   that.
3. Part survives DC at full power and fails on a pulsed profile at lower average
   power: steady-state R_theta is the wrong instrument whenever the pulse is shorter
   than tau. Check Z_theta(t) with the duty-cycle correction before re-sizing
   anything.
4. 1 device on a shared heatsink runs hot while its neighbours do not: compute the
   heatsink rise from the TOTAL dissipation before blaming that device's interface.
   Superposition gives every device the same SUM(P_i)*R_theta(sa) term.
5. Adding copper area has stopped helping: the bottleneck has moved to the z-axis.
   Count thermal vias before widening the plane, because through-plane FR4 is
   0.3 W/(m*K) and the in-plane spread has already saturated.
6. T_case matches prediction and the die still fails: the error is inside the package
   or in the power figure, not in the heatsink. Recompute P from electrical quantities
   measured AT temperature before re-sizing the sink.
7. An electrolytic capacitor is the first failure in a design whose semiconductors all
   sit within margin: measure its own local ambient rather than the board's. On
   Arrhenius it is the part whose life halves soonest, and it is usually sitting
   downstream of someone else's heatsink.
8. Solder joints cracking under a profile that Coffin-Manson says is safe: use delta_T
   at the JOINT rather than the ambient swing, and check the DNP of the package that
   actually failed. The largest part sees the largest strain for the same board
   delta_T.

[Known failure modes]
1. Computing T_j from nominal power at nominal ambient and never iterating, which
   excludes the positive temperature coefficient by construction.
2. Omitting R_theta(cs) because the part is bolted down. A dry, thin, pumped-out or
   voided TIM layer is frequently the largest term in the whole chain.
3. Using typical rather than maximum R_theta(jc) from the datasheet.
4. Reading the single-pulse Z_theta(t) curve as though it were the duty-cycle curve.
5. Analysing each device on a shared heatsink as though it were alone on it.
6. Taking FR4's in-plane conductivity for a through-board heat path.
7. Assuming an exposed pad is thermally connected when it is unsoldered, partially
   wetted or voided. The electrical connection can be perfect while the thermal one is
   absent.
8. Checking thermal stability only at the rated operating point, when runaway is a
   derivative condition and can bite below the rating.
9. Mixing a measured T_case with a datasheet R_theta(ja), which already contains the
   case-to-ambient path and therefore counts it twice.

[Exception catalogue]
1. The positive temperature coefficient that is a liability in a single device is the
   mechanism that makes paralleled MOSFETs share current: the hotter device's rising
   R_DS(on) diverts current to the cooler one. Reading HARD 3 as "positive tempco is a
   defect" would refuse a standard and correct design.
2. The SIGN in HARD 3 is device- and range-specific, never universal. An IGBT below
   its crossover point and a forward-biased diode both carry a NEGATIVE coefficient,
   which inverts the runaway inequality and the current-sharing argument together.
   Establish the coefficient over the actual operating range before applying either.
3. PTC heaters, self-regulating elements and foldback-limited regulators satisfy the
   runaway inequality deliberately and in a bounded way: the positive feedback IS the
   control loop. HARD 4 identifies an instability, and an instability with a saturating
   ceiling is a design rather than a fault.
4. For packages whose heat leaves through the leads and the board rather than the top
   — most QFN, SOIC and small exposed-pad parts with no usable case path — R_theta(ja)
   on a declared JEDEC board governs, and building the jc-cs-sa chain of HARD 1
   produces a confident wrong answer against a case path that does not exist.
5. Pulse widths far shorter than every tau make the steady-state network of HARD 1
   inapplicable, and a design that violates its steady-state limits can be entirely
   correct under Z_theta(t). Symmetrically, for pulses far longer than every tau the
   transient machinery is unnecessary and the steady-state sum is exact.
6. Coffin-Manson cycle life is not the limiting mechanism for a part that sees very
   few deep thermal cycles across its service life. A continuously powered device
   wears out by time at temperature — intermetallic growth, electrolyte loss,
   electromigration — so demanding a cycle count there answers the wrong question.

[Tool limitations]
1. A CFD or FEA thermal solve takes dissipation as an INPUT, so an uncoupled run
   cannot detect thermal runaway at all: it will report a comfortable temperature for
   a design that diverges. Only a solve with the electrical temperature dependence fed
   back can see the loop.
2. A thermal camera measures surface radiance and needs a correct emissivity per
   surface. Bare copper and solder read tens of degrees low, and the hottest point in
   the system is a die under a package the camera cannot see at all.
3. A datasheet R_theta(ja) belongs to a specific JEDEC test board in still air. It is
   a comparison figure between parts, not a prediction for another board, and using it
   as a design number is the commonest overestimate of available margin.
4. Published Z_theta(t) curves are single-pulse and drawn at a fixed set of duty
   cycles. Interpolating between them is an approximation with no stated error, and
   extrapolating below the shortest plotted pulse has no basis at all.
5. MIL-HDBK-217 returns a failure rate from a parts count and is not predictive in
   absolute terms. It can rank 2 designs against each other; it cannot be quoted as a
   service life.

[Limitations]
1. Does not cover liquid cooling (cold plates, microchannels, pumped loops).
2. Does not cover phase-change cooling (heat pipes, vapour chambers).
3. Does not cover RF power amplifier thermal management.
4. Does not cover cryogenic electronics or superconducting systems.
5. CFD/FEA is verification, not substitute for analytical bounds.
