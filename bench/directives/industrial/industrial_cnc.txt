DOMAIN: CNC Machining (Milling and Turning)
STANDARDS: ISO 286 (tolerances), ISO 1302 (surface finish), ISO 230 (machine tool testing), tool manufacturer datasheets
VERSION: 1.0
SCOPE: 3-axis milling and 2-axis turning of metals — conventional subtractive machining with carbide tooling

[HARD constraints — non-negotiable]
- Cutting speed: v_c = pi * D * n / 1000 (m/min), where D = tool/workpiece diameter (mm), n = spindle speed (rpm); must be within tool manufacturer's recommended range for the work material; typical carbide ranges: aluminium 200-600 m/min, mild steel 150-250 m/min, stainless 304 80-140 m/min, titanium Ti-6Al-4V 40-70 m/min
- Feed per tooth f_z (mm/tooth) must stay within manufacturer's range; exceeding maximum causes premature tool failure via edge chipping; too low causes rubbing, work hardening (especially austenitic stainless, Inconel), and accelerated flank wear
- Minimum chip thickness: h_min ~ 0.25-0.33 * r_edge (cutting edge radius) for carbide tools; below this threshold, ploughing occurs instead of cutting — generates heat without material removal; typically h_min > 0.01 mm for standard carbide inserts
- Tool deflection (end mill as cantilever): delta = (F * L^3) / (3 * E * I), where I = pi * d^4 / 64 for solid round tool; deflection must be < 1/3 of part tolerance; E_carbide ~ 580 GPa, E_HSS ~ 210 GPa
- Specific cutting force k_c (N/mm^2) determines cutting forces: F_c = k_c * a_p * f_z * z; typical k_c values: aluminium 700-900, mild steel 1800-2200, stainless 304 2400-2800, titanium 1400-1800; actual k_c varies with chip thickness (k_c = k_c1.1 * h^(-m_c) per Kienzle model)
- Surface finish (turning): R_a ~ f^2 / (32 * r_epsilon), where f = feed per revolution (mm/rev), r_epsilon = tool nose radius (mm); specified R_a constrains maximum permissible feed rate; R_a 1.6 um with r_epsilon 0.8 mm requires f <= 0.20 mm/rev
- Workholding force must exceed cutting forces with safety factor >= 2.0; for chuck work: F_grip * mu >= F_c * SF, where mu = friction coefficient (0.1-0.3 depending on jaw type and surface)
- Machine tool rigidity limits depth of cut for thin-walled parts; wall deflection under cutting force must be < tolerance; critical for aerospace parts with wall thickness < 2 mm (use backing supports, reduced a_p, increased number of passes)

[SOFT constraints — negotiable]
- Prefer climb (down) milling over conventional (up) milling: better surface finish, lower cutting forces, improved tool life (chip starts thick, exits thin — avoids rubbing at entry)
- Prefer standard catalogue tooling over specials (shorter lead time, lower cost, established cutting data)
- Minimise number of setups to reduce cumulative positioning error and setup time; 3-axis parts ideally 1-2 setups
- Prefer coated carbide inserts (TiAlN, AlCrN) for steel and stainless; uncoated or DLC-coated for aluminium (prevents built-up edge)
- Use high-pressure coolant (70+ bar) for deep hole drilling and difficult-to-machine materials (titanium, Inconel)

[Verification procedures]
- Verify all cutting parameters are within tool manufacturer's published data for the specific work material and operation
- Calculate tool deflection for longest unsupported tool length at maximum radial engagement; confirm delta < tolerance/3
- Check workholding force adequacy against maximum cutting forces, including safety factor; verify no part movement during interrupted cuts (e.g., keyway milling)
- Confirm post-processor output matches programmed feeds and speeds (machine-specific G-code verification)
- Validate that thermal expansion is accounted for in tight-tolerance work (steel grows ~12 um/m per degree C; aluminium ~23 um/m per degree C)
- Check tool life expectations against batch size; plan tool change points to avoid mid-feature tool failure

[Diagnostic heuristics]
- If every part in a batch is out of tolerance in the same direction by a similar amount, the cause is a systematic offset — tool length or diameter offset, probe calibration, work offset — not the cutting process; check the offset table and a first-off measurement before changing feeds and speeds
- If the error scatters unit to unit with no trend, look at loading repeatability (chip trapped under a locator, inconsistent clamp torque, burr on a datum face) before machine positioning accuracy; random part-to-part error is almost never the ballscrew
- If a dimension drifts steadily through a batch, read the SHAPE of the drift: a step at each tool change is tool wear, a drift that follows warm-up and then plateaus is thermal growth, and a drift that continues indefinitely is neither — establish which before touching the programme
- If a surface carries regular marks, count them against spindle revolutions and against tooth count before condemning the insert: 1 mark per revolution is runout or a single high tooth, marks at tooth-passing frequency are the normal feed scallop, and chatter marks sit at neither frequency, near a structural natural frequency instead
- If achieved R_a is worse than f^2 / (32 * r_epsilon) predicts, check for built-up edge and tool runout before reducing feed; the equation is a geometric floor that assumes a sharp tool running true, and on aluminium a lower feed can make built-up edge worse rather than better
- If chatter appears, change spindle speed before stiffening the setup: regenerative chatter is speed-dependent and a 10-20% change in n often removes it, whereas a stiffness change means a different tool, holder or fixture
- If a bore passes a diameter check but the mating part will not assemble, check form and the measurement datum before size: a 2-point diameter cannot see lobing or taper, and an ISO 286 size tolerance does not control form
- If a thin-walled part measures correctly clamped and fails released, the part is springing; measure free-state and quantify clamp-induced distortion before changing the toolpath

[Known failure modes]
- Tolerance stack-up across setups treated as the single-feature tolerance: 3 setups each holding +/-0.05 mm can put +/-0.15 mm between features machined in different setups, while the drawing tolerances the RELATIONSHIP rather than each setup
- Worst-case stack applied where contributors are independent and statistical, or root-sum-square applied where they are not independent (a common fixture error appears in every contributor at once)
- Programme datum scheme different from the drawing's datum reference frame, so the part is machined accurately to the wrong reference
- Catalogue cutting data applied to a different condition of the same material (cold-drawn versus annealed, free-machining versus standard, work-hardened skin on austenitic stainless)
- Coolant concentration never checked with a refractometer, so tool life and surface finish drift between batches with no recorded cause
- Spindle and machine warm-up skipped, so the first parts of a shift differ systematically from the rest of the batch
- Cutting to the nominal dimension where the fit is unilateral (an H7 bore, a shaft at h6), instead of aiming at the middle of the tolerance band and leaving room for tool wear
- Parts measured at an unrecorded temperature and compared against a tolerance referenced to 20 C per ISO 1; steel moves ~12 um/m per degree C, so a 300 mm feature moves 3.6 um per degree C
- First-off inspected and the batch never re-checked, so a drift is discovered after the last part is cut

[Exception catalogue]
- The h_min floor scales with EDGE RADIUS, not with the material: a ground HSS, PCD or polished-edge tool with r_edge of 1-5 um has h_min of roughly 0.25-1.6 um, so chip loads far below the 0.01 mm figure quoted for standard carbide inserts are correct cutting rather than ploughing
- R_a ~ f^2 / (32 * r_epsilon) is a turning relation for a conventional nose radius; a wiper insert's flat trailing edge deliberately breaks it and holds the same R_a at roughly double the feed, and the relation does not describe milling at all (where stepover and tool radius set the scallop), nor ground or burnished surfaces
- The deflection criterion applies to the UNCOMPENSATED residual: where a long-reach tool's deflection is known, repeatable and offset in the toolpath, or removed by spring passes, the raw delta may exceed tolerance/3 while the finished feature does not
- The climb-milling preference inverts in 2 identifiable cases: on a machine with measurable backlash in the feed axis, where climb milling pulls the work into the cutter, and through an abrasive skin (castings, flame-cut edges, forging scale) where the entry condition rather than the exit condition governs tool life
- F_grip * mu >= F_c * SF assumes friction resists the cut. Where the part bears against a positive stop, dowel or shoulder in the cutting direction, friction is not the governing term; vacuum and magnetic chucks are instead bounded by holding pressure times effective sealed or pole area
- Thermal compensation is about DIFFERENTIAL temperature. A fit between 2 parts of the same material at the same temperature is self-compensating, and correcting one of them for expansion introduces the very error the correction was meant to remove

[Tool limitations]
- CAM software verifies geometry and collisions, not force: a toolpath that simulates cleanly can still deflect, chatter or break the tool, because the simulation carries no k_c, no stiffness and no damping
- A CMM reports what it probed and how it was fitted: a 2-point or least-squares diameter carries no information about lobing or taper, and switching between least-squares and minimum-circumscribed fitting changes the reported size by more than the machine's repeatability
- ISO 230 accuracy figures characterise the machine unloaded; they exclude tool deflection, thermal drift under cutting load and workholding error, which are usually the larger contributors to the error on the part
- A published tool life is for a stated material, coolant and engagement, and the Taylor exponents for the actual combination are generally unavailable, so a predicted life carries no stated uncertainty
- A roughness tester reports R_a over a chosen cut-off and filter; the same surface measured at a different cut-off returns a different number, and R_a cannot distinguish a uniformly smooth surface from one with isolated deep scratches — R_z or R_max is needed for that

[Limitations]
- Does not cover 4-axis or 5-axis simultaneous machining (different toolpath strategies, RTCP/TCP compensation)
- Does not cover EDM (wire or sinker) — different material removal mechanism (no cutting forces)
- Does not cover grinding, honing, or lapping (different surface generation physics)
- Does not cover additive or hybrid (additive + subtractive) manufacturing
- Does not cover Swiss-type (sliding headstock) turning for small-diameter long parts
- Does not cover high-speed machining (HSM) strategies where spindle dynamics and toolpath continuity dominate
- Does not cover cryogenic machining or minimum quantity lubrication (MQL) — different thermal management assumptions
- Does not cover non-metallic machining (composites, ceramics, plastics — different failure modes and tool geometries)
