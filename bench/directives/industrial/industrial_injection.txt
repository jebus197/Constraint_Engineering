DOMAIN: Thermoplastic Injection Moulding
STANDARDS: ISO 294, ISO 20457 (formerly DIN 16742), material supplier datasheets
VERSION: 1.0
SCOPE: Single-component thermoplastic injection moulding — conventional process, steel tooling

[HARD constraints — non-negotiable]
- Melt temperature must be within material supplier's recommended processing window: ABS 220-260 C, PA66 275-295 C, PC 280-320 C, PP 200-250 C, POM 190-210 C; exceeding upper limit causes thermal degradation, below lower limit causes short shots and high viscosity
- Mould temperature affects crystallinity, shrinkage, and surface finish; must be actively controlled: PP 40-80 C, PA66 80-100 C (dry) or 40-60 C (conditioned), PC 80-120 C, ABS 50-80 C
- Shrinkage is material-specific and anisotropic (flow vs cross-flow differ): PP 1.5-2.0%, ABS 0.4-0.7%, PA66 0.8-1.5% (dry, unfilled) or 0.3-0.5% (30% GF), PC 0.5-0.7%; tooling dimensions must compensate: D_tool = D_part * (1 + S) where S is shrinkage fraction
- Wall thickness must be uniform (+/- 10% variation maximum) to prevent sink marks, warpage, and differential cooling; transitions must be gradual (taper ratio 3:1 minimum)
- Draft angle >= 0.5 deg per side minimum for untextured surfaces; >= 1.5 deg per side for textured surfaces; add 1 deg per 0.025 mm (0.001 in) of texture depth (VDI/SPI standard)
- Gate location affects weld line position; weld lines must NOT coincide with high-stress areas (weld line strength typically 60-90% of base material) or Class A cosmetic surfaces
- Injection pressure for runner flow: P = (128 * eta * L * Q) / (pi * d^4) for circular runner (Hagen-Poiseuille); actual cavity fill pressure typically 30-80 MPa depending on part geometry and wall thickness
- Clamping force: F_clamp > P_cavity_max * A_projected; rule of thumb 3-5 tonnes/cm^2 of projected area for most unfilled thermoplastics, 5-7 tonnes/cm^2 for glass-filled; insufficient clamp force causes flash
- Residence time in barrel must not exceed material's thermal stability limit (typically 5-10 min for PA66, 8-15 min for PC); excessive residence causes degradation, discolouration, reduced mechanical properties

[SOFT constraints — negotiable]
- Prefer single-cavity tooling for prototype and low volume (< 10k parts/year); multi-cavity for production (amortises tool cost)
- Prefer hot runner systems over cold runner for high-volume production (reduces material waste 10-30%, eliminates regrind variation)
- Design for automated ejection (avoid undercuts requiring side actions where possible)
- Prefer direct/edge gates for structural parts; pin-point gates for cosmetic parts (smaller witness mark)
- Target cycle time optimisation: cooling time typically 50-70% of total cycle; t_cool ~ s^2 / (pi^2 * alpha) * ln((4/pi) * (T_melt - T_mould) / (T_eject - T_mould)) where s = wall thickness, alpha = thermal diffusivity

[Verification procedures]
- Run mould flow simulation (Moldflow, Moldex3D, or equivalent) to verify fill pattern, weld line locations, and air trap positions before tool steel is cut
- Verify clamp tonnage against projected area at maximum expected cavity pressure (include runner system projected area)
- Check cooling channel layout gives uniform mould surface temperature (delta T_mould < 10 C across cavity)
- Confirm ejector pin positions do not mar cosmetic surfaces and provide balanced ejection force
- Validate gate size: too small causes gate freeze-off before packing; too large causes long cycle time and gate vestige
- Check that venting is adequate (vent depth 0.02-0.05 mm for most thermoplastics, 0.01-0.02 mm for nylons to prevent flash)

[Diagnostic heuristics]
- If the part is short at the end of fill, confirm the shot is being delivered — cushion present and repeatable, non-return valve sealing — before raising melt temperature; a worn check ring loses pressure during hold and shows as short shots AND shot-to-shot weight variation together
- If part weight varies shot to shot with every setting constant, look at cushion and screw recovery repeatability before the process window; a cushion reaching zero means the part is being filled against a volumetric stop rather than packed
- If a sink mark sits opposite a rib or boss, the cause is local thickness ratio, not pack pressure: a rib base above roughly 0.6 of nominal wall sinks whatever the hold profile does
- If flash appears on one side of the tool only, check platen parallelism, tie-bar strain balance and shut-off wear before adding tonnage; adding tonnage to chase one-sided flash crushes the shut-off and makes the flash permanent
- If a dimension measured at the press disagrees with the inspection bench, fix a conditioning time and measure to it: semi-crystalline parts go on shrinking for 24 hours or more, and PA66 grows as it takes up moisture
- If a part with uniform walls still warps, compare the 2 mould halves' surface temperatures before touching the shrinkage allowance: a delta T across the cavity bends the part toward the hotter side with no help from the material
- If a defect sits exactly at the last place to fill, read it as venting, because trapped air compresses and burns there; a void at the thickest section instead is a shrinkage void, and sectioning the part separates the 2 by POSITION rather than by appearance
- If a glass-filled part holds tolerance along the flow and loses it across the flow, the fill pattern or gating has changed, not the shrinkage allowance; confirm the fill pattern before re-cutting steel

[Known failure modes]
- Tool steel cut to the middle of a published shrinkage range rather than steel-safe, so correcting the first article means adding metal by welding instead of removing it
- Shrinkage taken as one number for a material family, when filler content, wall thickness, hold pressure and mould temperature each move it within the published range
- Machined-metal tolerances applied to a moulding; ISO 20457 exists because tool tolerance, shrinkage variation and process variation stack, and a general +/-0.05 mm on a 150 mm moulded dimension is not achievable
- Fibre orientation ignored in structural analysis, so a glass-filled part is modelled with isotropic bulk properties that overstate stiffness and strength across the flow direction
- Regrind added at an unrecorded ratio, so molecular weight, colour and impact properties drift between batches with no traceable cause
- Drying verified by hopper residence time rather than by a moisture measurement; PA and PET hydrolyse at melt temperature and the damage is invisible in the moulded part
- Cycle shortened by cutting cooling time, so the part is ejected above its heat deflection temperature and distorts on the conveyor rather than in the tool
- Process window established in the first 20-30 shots, before the tool reaches thermal steady state, so the settings do not hold once production is running
- Ejector force raised to overcome insufficient draft, which polishes, then galls, then scores the cavity wall

[Exception catalogue]
- Draft is a STRAIGHT-PULL requirement. Zero-draft and undercut faces are routine where the tool carries a side action, lifter, collapsing core or unscrewing core, and shallow undercuts in flexible materials are deliberately stripped off the core — so a zero-draft face is a tooling decision, not a violation
- The uniform-wall rule is for conventionally cooled solid sections. Structural foam, gas-assist and physically or chemically foamed parts deliberately run thick sections with a cellular core, and a lens or light guide runs very thick on purpose with a long slow pack and extended cooling
- Residence time must be COMPUTED from shot size and barrel capacity, not read off the datasheet: a part using a small fraction of the barrel reaches the degradation limit in far less wall-clock time than the published minutes, while a part using most of the barrel has more headroom than the figure suggests
- D_tool = D_part * (1 + S) is a free-shrinkage scale. A dimension constrained by steel through cooling — an internal diameter around a core, a feature between 2 shut-offs — does not shrink freely, and applying the full factor there over-compensates
- The 3-5 tonnes/cm^2 projected-area heuristic is a filled-cavity-at-full-pack estimate. It over-predicts for a thin centrally gated part filled at low pressure and under-predicts for a long thin high-viscosity flow path, and injection-compression moves the requirement off projected area entirely
- Mould temperature below the published window is correct where LOW crystallinity is the goal: clarified PP, amorphous PET and high-gloss skins are deliberately quenched, trading post-mould shrinkage and dimensional stability for clarity or surface

[Tool limitations]
- Mould-flow simulation predicts fill pattern, weld-line position and air traps well, and warpage much less well, because warpage depends on fibre orientation, crystallisation kinetics and residual stress rather than on flow alone
- A simulation's material card is a fitted viscosity and pvT model for a nominal grade; a different lot, a colour masterbatch or any regrind changes the rheology, and the card does not know it has
- Closed-form cooling time assumes 1-D conduction through a uniform wall into a mould face at constant temperature, so it is wrong at exactly the places that end the cycle: corners, ribs, bosses and anywhere a core is thermally isolated
- A micrometer or touch-probe CMM applies force to a compliant moulding and the reading includes the deflection; optical and scanning methods avoid it but struggle on translucent and high-gloss surfaces
- A cavity pressure sensor reports the pressure AT THE SENSOR: a single gate-side sensor cannot distinguish gate freeze-off from a pack shortfall at the far end of the cavity

[Limitations]
- Does not cover thermoset moulding (different cure chemistry, no remelt, different tooling requirements)
- Does not cover multi-component or overmoulding processes (sequential or rotary)
- Does not cover micro-moulding (parts < 1g, features < 0.1mm — different flow physics)
- Does not cover gas-assist or water-assist injection moulding
- Does not cover metal injection moulding (MIM) or ceramic injection moulding (CIM)
- Does not cover in-mould decoration (IMD), in-mould labelling (IML), or insert moulding
- Does not cover liquid silicone rubber (LSR) injection moulding (different cure mechanism, different tooling requirements)
