DOMAIN: Mathematics — Applied and Modelling
SCOPE: Model formulation, dimensional analysis, asymptotics and scaling,
       optimisation, differential-equation modelling, sensitivity
VERSION: 1.0
TIER: DRAFT
SEVERITY SCALE: none of its own. Applied mathematics INHERITS the consequence
       scale of the domain it is applied in, and that inheritance is the whole
       point: the same model error is epistemic in one setting and lethal in
       another. Where the application is engineering, FMEA severity applies
       with critical at 7; where clinical, CTCAE with critical at Grade 3.
SOURCES: SI Brochure 9th edition (BIPM, 2019) for units.

[HARD constraints — non-negotiable]

Dimensional homogeneity on every equation, checked with pint rather than by
  eye. Homogeneity is necessary and NOT sufficient -- a missing factor of 2
  survives it -- so a pass is the absence of one disproof, not evidence.

Every modelling assumption must be stated and its violation consequence named.
  An unstated assumption is indistinguishable from an error when the model
  disagrees with reality.

The regime of validity must be declared: linear, small-amplitude, continuum,
  quasi-static, dilute. A result used outside its regime is not approximate, it
  is inapplicable.

Boundary and initial conditions must be sufficient and consistent. An
  underdetermined problem has many solutions and a solver will return one of
  them without complaint; an overdetermined one has none and the solver will
  return the least-squares answer as though it were a solution.

Conservation and positivity must be checked on the OUTPUT, not assumed from
  the formulation. A discretised conservation law can fail to conserve, and a
  concentration can go negative, without any step looking wrong.

A fitted parameter is not a measured one. Reporting a fit coefficient without
  its uncertainty and without the fit's residual structure overstates what was
  learned.

Sensitivity must be reported where the conclusion depends on an estimated
  input. If the answer moves materially within the input's plausible range,
  the answer is the range.

[SOFT constraints — negotiable]

Prefer non-dimensionalisation before solving; it reveals which groups matter
  and reduces the parameter count.
Prefer the simplest model that reproduces the behaviour of interest, and state
  what it omits.
Prefer an asymptotic result with a stated regime over a numerical one without.
State the direction of any approximation: does it over- or under-estimate?
Prefer a scaling argument as a cross-check on any detailed computation.

[Verification procedures]

pint on every expression; astropy.units where astronomical.
SymPy for the symbolic reduction and the asymptotic expansion.
scipy for the numerical solution, with a second method as the cross-check --
  2 integrators, or an integrator against a closed form.
Limiting cases by substitution, executed: each limit should reduce to a known
  result, and the ones that do not localise the error.
Sensitivity by execution: perturb each input across its plausible range and
  report the output range, rather than asserting robustness.
Conservation check on the computed solution, reported as a residual.

[Diagnostic heuristics]

If the model is insensitive to a parameter it should depend on, a term was
  dropped or the regime makes it negligible -- determine which.
If a dimensionless group is far from 1, a simplification is available and
  probably intended.
If the numerical and asymptotic answers disagree, check the regime before
  checking the arithmetic.
If a fit is excellent and the residuals are structured, the model is wrong in
  a way the fit statistic cannot see.
An optimisation that lands exactly on a bound is reporting the bound, not an
  optimum.

[Known failure modes]

Carrying an approximation past its regime without flagging it.
Fitting more parameters than the data can identify, which produces a confident
  answer with no information in it.
Treating a correlation in the fit as a relationship in the system.
Omitting a boundary condition and accepting whichever solution the solver
  returns.
Reporting a point optimum where the objective is flat, so any nearby point is
  equally good and the precision is fictitious.
Using a steady-state model for a transient.
Reporting a trend rather than the level, since a trend claim is a claim about
  the next measurement.

[Exception catalogue]

Deliberately crude models are legitimate for order-of-magnitude work, and
  demanding precision from one is a category error.
Chaotic systems have bounded predictability by construction; a forecast
  diverging is the physics, not the method.
Stiff systems need implicit methods and an explicit one will appear unstable
  rather than wrong.
Inverse problems are often ill-posed, so regularisation is required and the
  regularisation choice is part of the result.
Where no formal conversion exists between 2 quantities, they must be reported
  SEPARATELY in their own scales rather than combined -- a sum of 1 inch and
  1 centimetre composes because the conversion is defined, and a sum of 2
  unrelated indices does not.

[Tool limitations]

A solver returns an answer for an ill-posed problem without indicating that it
  is ill-posed.
Automatic differentiation is exact for the code written, which may not be the
  model intended.
pint cannot catch an error in a dimensionless group, nor refuse a sum of 2
  dimensionless quantities.
An optimiser reports a local optimum and calls it a solution; global claims
  need their own argument.
