DOMAIN: Mathematics — General & Applied
SCOPE: Calculus, optimisation, combinatorics, linear algebra, probability,
       differential equations, numerical methods
VERSION: 1.0

[HARD constraints — non-negotiable]

Arithmetic consistency: every intermediate calculation must be verifiable
  step-by-step. No skipped steps in derivations presented as rigorous.

Definitional precision: "half full" means half the total volume, not half
  the height (unless the container has uniform cross-section). "Maximum"
  requires second-order verification. "Minimum" requires second-order
  verification. Extremum claims without sufficiency conditions are incomplete.

Substitution boundary tracking: when changing variables in definite integrals,
  transform BOTH the integrand AND the limits. Verify transformed limits by
  evaluating the substitution function at the original limits.

Inclusion-exclusion: for n sets, the formula alternates signs through all
  intersection orders. Verify by partition: the sum of all disjoint regions
  must equal the universal set (or stated total).

Dimensional consistency: in applied problems, every term in an equation must
  have the same units. Cross-check final answers against dimensional analysis.

Division by zero: verify that no denominator in the solution equals zero at
  the evaluation point. Flag removable singularities explicitly.

Domain restrictions: log(x) requires x > 0. sqrt(x) requires x >= 0 (reals).
  arcsin(x) requires |x| <= 1. tan(x) undefined at odd multiples of π/2.

Convergence: infinite series and improper integrals require explicit
  convergence verification before evaluation.

[SOFT constraints — negotiable]

Prefer closed-form solutions over numerical approximations when tractable.
State numerical precision (decimal places or significant figures) explicitly.
Use standard notation: ∫, Σ, lim, d/dx. Define non-standard symbols.
When multiple solution methods exist, cross-verify with at least one
  alternative method or known result.

[Verification procedures]

Back-substitution: plug the solution back into the original equation/constraint.
Boundary checks: verify solutions at domain boundaries and critical points.
Limiting cases: check that the solution behaves correctly in extreme cases
  (e.g., as a parameter → 0 or → ∞).
Consistency partitions: for counting problems, verify that disjoint subsets
  sum to the known total.
Sign checks: verify that physical quantities (lengths, areas, volumes, rates)
  are non-negative where required.

[Diagnostic heuristics]

If an extremum was reported from a vanishing first derivative alone, the
  second-order condition is the first thing to check: a maximum, a minimum and
  an inflection all have a stationary point, and the first-order equations
  cannot tell them apart.
If a claimed maximum sits on the boundary of the domain, the stationary-point
  machinery never applied there at all; compare interior and boundary
  separately.
If a definite integral disagrees between substitution and integration by parts,
  check the transformed LIMITS before re-checking the antiderivative. The
  integrand gets transformed and the limits get forgotten.
If a probability exceeds 1, the cases overlap; if supposedly disjoint cases
  fall short of the stated total, a case is missing. The 2 observables point at
  different repairs, and both are repartitions rather than adjustments to any
  single term.
If a count is off by a small integer or factorial factor, an ordering was
  imposed or removed: factors of 2, 6 and n! are a permutation counted once too
  often or once too few, not an arithmetic slip.
If a linear system returned a solution while the matrix is near-singular, check
  the rank and the consistency of the augmented system before claiming
  uniqueness: a least-squares answer arrives in the same shape as an exact one.
If an ODE solution satisfies the equation but not the data, the homogeneous part
  was dropped during simplification; a particular solution alone satisfies the
  equation and almost never the initial condition.
If the answer does not depend on a parameter the problem says it depends on,
  look for a cancellation that quietly assumed that parameter is nonzero.

[Known failure modes]

Changing variables in a definite integral and leaving the original limits.
Reporting a stationary point as an extremum with no second-order or boundary
  argument.
Cancelling or dividing by an expression that can vanish, which loses exactly
  the root where it does.
Stopping inclusion-exclusion at pairwise intersections, so triple overlaps are
  subtracted twice and added back never.
Conditioning errors: asserting P(A|B) = P(B|A), or multiplying probabilities of
  events that are not independent.
Evaluating a series or an improper integral before convergence is established,
  where a formal manipulation returns a plausible finite wrong answer.
Treating a necessary condition as sufficient -- Lagrange stationarity, the
  first-derivative test, a nonzero determinant.
Dropping the domain restriction a transformation introduced, by squaring both
  sides or by cancelling a logarithm.
Carrying an algebraic identity outside the region where both sides are defined,
  such as sqrt(x*y) = sqrt(x)*sqrt(y) with a negative factor present.

[Exception catalogue]

The real-domain restrictions are restrictions on the REALS. The logarithm of a
  negative number, the square root of a negative, and arcsin outside [-1, 1]
  are all defined over the complex numbers, where the constraint becomes a
  branch-cut question rather than a prohibition. Refusing the computation is
  wrong; failing to declare the branch is the actual error.
Divergence is a result. An infinite limit, a divergent series and an integral
  that does not converge are each complete answers, and the convergence
  requirement is discharged by establishing divergence just as well as by
  establishing convergence.
0/0 and the other indeterminate forms are not division-by-zero defects. They
  are instructions to take a limit, and a removable singularity has a value by
  continuous extension: sin(x)/x is 1 at 0.
A formally divergent series is legitimately summable under a DECLARED method --
  Abel, Cesaro, Borel -- and an asymptotic series is useful precisely where it
  diverges. The requirement is that the method be named, not that the series
  converge.
Dimensional consistency has no subject in a purely mathematical statement. A
  combinatorial identity, an inequality between dimensionless quantities or a
  bound on a ratio carries no units to check, so demanding them is a category
  error rather than rigour.
Where the objective is not differentiable, the second-order condition does not
  exist. A subgradient argument or a direct comparison is then the correct
  sufficiency test, and its absence is not a gap.

[Tool limitations]

An unevaluated return is not a result. SymPy handing back Integral(...),
  Sum(...) or an empty list from solve() is a statement about SymPy's methods
  and not about the mathematics: it has NOT shown that no closed form exists,
  and it has not shown that one does. The claim returns to unverified and needs
  a different route.
A constraint solver answering satisfiable has produced 1 witness. That settles
  an existence claim and says nothing about a universally quantified one: to use
  z3 on a "for all" statement, negate it, require unsat, and state the fragment
  and the bounds over which that unsat holds. unknown is a third answer and is
  neither of the other 2.
simplify is not canonical. 2 correct forms can both resist simplification while
  differing only in presentation, so subtract them and simplify the difference,
  or evaluate both at random points, before concluding that they differ.
Assumptions govern what is permitted. A symbol declared without positive=True
  carries the complex branch, so an expected simplification is refused and an
  unexpected one is allowed. A simplification that changes when an assumption
  changes was never an identity over the wider domain.
A numerical quadrature or root-finder reports an error estimate that assumes the
  integrand is as smooth as the rule requires. A singularity or an oscillation
  inside the interval breaks the estimate silently while the computation still
  returns a number.

[Limitations]

Does not cover abstract algebra, topology, or number theory beyond elementary.
Does not cover numerical stability analysis for iterative methods.
Not applicable to proof-based pure mathematics (theorem proving).
Does not address computational complexity of algorithms.
